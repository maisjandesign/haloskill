# Proposal narrative architecture

Choose only the sections needed to earn the client's next decision. A typical proposal is 8–14 slides, but clarity—not count—is the rule.

## Default sequence

1. **Cover — the opportunity in one line.** Name the client, project, proposal date, and proposal owner.
2. **Executive framing — what should change.** Summarize the client's situation, desired outcome, and why the work matters now.
3. **Goals and success conditions.** Use 3–5 observable outcomes. Do not invent KPIs.
4. **Recommended approach / roadmap.** Show phases as folder-tab tiles and identify the recommended path.
5. **Scope by phase.** Give each major phase its own step slide when it needs explanation: objective, activities, client inputs, deliverables, and duration.
6. **Deliverables.** Consolidate what is handed over, in which formats, and what is excluded.
7. **Timeline and dependencies.** Show phases, review points, client responsibilities, and schedule assumptions.
8. **Team and collaboration model.** Include only confirmed people/roles. Explain governance, cadence, and decision ownership.
9. **Relevant proof.** Use one or two cases that directly reduce the client's perceived risk; cite verified outcomes.
10. **Investment or options.** Make the recommended option visually primary. Specify currency, tax treatment, payment schedule, and validity.
11. **Assumptions and terms.** Keep concise: revisions, dependencies, out-of-scope work, IP, cancellation, and expenses only if supplied.
12. **Next step.** State the action, owner, and date; optionally add a verified testimonial or contact block.

## Editing logic

- Merge goals and framing when the brief is simple.
- Split scope into multiple slides when phases differ meaningfully.
- Omit team, proof, or terms if they add no decision value or the facts are unavailable.
- Place pricing after the client understands value, scope, and risk reduction.
- When pricing is intentionally withheld, replace the investment slide with “Commercial model” and explain the calculation method only if provided.
- Put detailed legal language in an appendix or a separate document; keep the main deck decision-oriented.

## Slide writing pattern

For each slide define:

1. The decision or belief it should create.
2. A headline that states that takeaway.
3. The minimum evidence needed to support it.
4. One visual form that makes the evidence easier to scan.
5. The next logical question answered by the following slide.

## Commercial integrity

- Never infer a budget from company size or industry.
- Never label a scope “fixed” unless the user states that it is fixed.
- Never invent turnaround time, revision rounds, staffing, discounts, or bonuses.
- Distinguish estimates from commitments.
- When totals are user-provided, recalculate them and flag discrepancies before authoring.
- Keep optional work clearly separate from the base scope.
