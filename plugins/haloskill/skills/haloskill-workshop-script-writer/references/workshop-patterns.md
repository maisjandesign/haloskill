# Workshop structure and worked examples

This is preparation guidance. The stage map and teaching notes do not belong in the spoken script; the numbered blocks in the complete example do. The scenarios below demonstrate writing technique, not verified facts about the next client. No example substitutes for that client's research.

## Stage map

| Stage | What to find out | Decision to capture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Website goal | Which commercial change the site must support; what is still unresolved after sales | Priority outcome and what a useful conversion means |
| 2 Visitor situation | What triggers interest, how the visitor handles it today, who chooses | One priority situation and the people involved |
| 3 Reasons to choose | Why change or choose this offer; what makes the promise credible | Lead benefit, meaningful difference and available proof |
| 4 Questions and objections | What stops action; which explanation would help | Essential answers, evidence and limits of claims |
| 5 Voice | How to express the value naturally for this audience | Concrete language choices, not a list of adjectives |
| 6 Discovery and action | What visitors know on arrival and the useful next step | Entry context, key information and action; search hypotheses for later validation |

Existing mission and vision are inputs. Do not run a brand-strategy workshop from scratch. Do not turn Stage 6 into an SEO audit or migration planning session.

## Opening pattern

Greet → acknowledge established goal → explain today's useful decision → explain that prepared ideas can be challenged → begin. Roughly 80–140 words is often enough, not a quota. Check project status only if it materially changes the discussion.

## Complete stage example — required output structure

Use this visible format for every stage: stage title → brief purpose → three numbered blocks. Each block contains a prepared setup, one main question, and one follow-up beside it. The purpose should be short enough to read aloud. The numbers and Follow-up labels are navigation; the remaining text is ready to say. Do not print “setup”, “structure” or “example” in a client script.

Teaching input: sales has established that the website should help win partner meetings. The three open decisions below are the trigger for change, the visitor's decision before a meeting, and the sign of a useful meeting. These are example inputs, not facts to copy into a future client's script.

---

### Stage 1 — What the website needs to change

First, let's look at what you need from the new website. This will help us decide what the content should focus on.

#### 1. Why now

You mentioned that the website should help your sales team get a first meeting.

One possibility is that the current site does not give potential partners enough confidence to reply. Another is that your sales approach has changed, and the website needs to catch up.

**What has made this a priority now?**

**Follow-up:** Can you describe a recent situation that made the need clear?

#### 2. Before the meeting

Let's look at the moment before someone agrees to a call.

Imagine a potential partner has received your email and opened the website. They may only spend a minute there before deciding whether to continue the conversation.

**What would they need to understand in that minute to feel a meeting is worth their time?**

**Follow-up:** What do potential partners usually ask before agreeing to meet?

#### 3. A useful result

It would also help to define what a good result looks like for your team.

More meetings may be useful. But the content could also help people understand the offer before they speak to you, so the first conversation starts further along.

**What would be different in a first meeting if the website had done its job well?**

**Follow-up:** What would you want to spend less time explaining on that call?

---

The blocks move from the reason for change to the visitor's understanding to a useful business result. They do not ask the same question three ways. Only the first block repeats the established sales goal. Each setup works without knowing the client's earlier live answers. Tailor the three decisions to what remains unresolved for the actual client.

## Worked passage B — translate evidence into a useful choice

Teaching input: verified product materials distinguish the parent's interface from what runs on a child's device. Buyers may miss that distinction.

Weak:
> How does the product work? What about the app, privacy, integration, settings, hosting, and limitations?

Prepared spoken passage:
> There is one detail I would like to make easier to understand.
>
> Your materials describe an app for parents, while the protection does not require an app on the child's phone.
>
> To someone seeing the product for the first time, those two points could seem to conflict.
>
> I think a simple picture could help: what the parent uses, what the child uses, and where the protection happens.
>
> How would you explain that to a partner without a technical background?

> **Follow-up:** Which part of that explanation do partners most often misunderstand?

This is one question block, not a complete stage. Verify the product details afresh before using this pattern for any real client.

## Pacing and follow-ups

Give important setups enough room to show the thinking, often 3–6 short sentences. Later questions can be direct; do not force another research preamble onto every turn. Keep all three question blocks visually separate. Each needs a relevant setup, but a later setup can be a short transition rather than another full research summary.

Prepare exactly one follow-up immediately after each main question: three per stage. The facilitator asks it only if the answer needs depth or clarification; skip it when already answered. Selective use during the call does not mean omitting follow-ups from the prepared script. A necessary decision belongs in the main conversation; a nice-to-have detail can stay in preparation notes. Do not disguise a question bank as “prompts”, “probes” or “things to cover”.

## Check before delivery

- Does every stage contain three numbered question blocks, each with a setup, one main question and one adjacent follow-up?
- Could the facilitator read this as written, in natural English?
- Does the setup explain why the question matters, using client evidence or a clearly tentative situation?
- Can the client answer the main question without remembering three other questions?
- Is each fact introduced once, with transitions that move the discussion forward?
- Are source notes, structure instructions and technical administration outside the spoken text?
- Does the language sound like the original voice reference rather than a consultant questionnaire?
